# Perfect Competition — Engagement Brief

## 1. The Problem

The farm needs to decide how to use its 64 available beds between tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun to make the most profit. From my background in the Marine Corps as a senior enlisted leader for more than 20 years, I look at this as a resource-allocation decision: we have limited people, time, and space, so we need to put those resources where they produce the best return.

The farm does not control the market price, making it a price taker. Tomatoes bring $8,800 per bed, carrots $2,094, and mesclun $2,700. Each crop is also limited to 20 beds. Labor is another major constraint because the labor required increases as more beds of the same crop are planted.

The goal is to balance revenue with the increasing cost of labor. My HR and leadership background also makes me view labor as a limited resource that must be used efficiently, rather than assuming that producing more of the highest-priced crop is always better.

## 2. My Hypothesis

I expect the optimal mix to be about 15 tomato beds, 20 carrot beds, and 20 mesclun beds.

I expect tomatoes to receive the most attention because they generate significantly more revenue per bed. However, their 10% diminishing-return rate is much higher than carrots at 2.5% and mesclun at 1.25%. I believe rising labor costs will eventually make additional tomato beds less attractive, while carrots and mesclun can continue producing value at their caps.

I estimate the tipping point falls around bed 15 for tomatoes, since a 10% compounding labor penalty applied to an $8,800/bed crop should cross the marginal-cost line meaningfully earlier than the lower-value, lower-penalty crops hitting their caps.

The remaining 9 beds are left unplanted in this hypothesis, since I expect the labor cost of a 21st+ tomato bed to exceed its revenue once diminishing returns compound — carrots and mesclun are already at their 20-bed caps.

My hypothesis will be proven wrong if the model produces a substantially different mix. The key factor I expect to determine the decision is marginal cost versus the market price of each crop.
