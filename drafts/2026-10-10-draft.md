# Draft 1 — Leasing Solar in Hawaiʻi

## 1. The Challenge

Electricity is a major expense for families living in Hawaiʻi. With electricity prices being so high, homeowners have to find ways to manage their monthly bills while still meeting their household needs. This is especially important for families who use a lot of electricity or want to save money for other expenses. Solar energy is one option, but deciding whether to lease or buy a system can be difficult because there are long-term costs to consider.

I wanted to research this topic because I have solar at my own home and have seen a difference in my monthly expenses. In December 2023, I paid $362.59 for electricity and $136.59 for my original Sunrun system, totaling $499.18. My current combined monthly cost is about $355.30. Although this is a decrease, I still have to account for my new solar lease and its yearly payment increases. My goal is to determine whether leasing solar is a good financial decision over 25 years and whether the same option would make sense for other homeowners in Hawaiʻi.

## 2. Economic Concepts

Three economic concepts that apply to my solar lease are time value of money, substitutes, and inflation. The time value of money means that money today is worth more than the same amount in the future. With my solar lease, I make payments every month, but the savings I receive from lower electricity costs happen over time. I need to determine whether those savings will be worth the total cost of the lease over 25 years.

Substitutes also apply because leasing solar is not my only option. I could continue buying electricity from Hawaiian Electric or consider purchasing my own solar system. These are realistic alternatives because each option provides a way to meet my household's electricity needs, but the costs and financial risks are different.

Inflation is another important factor. Electricity rates can increase over time, which could make solar more valuable. However, my lease payment also increases by about 2.99% annually. I need to compare both increases to understand whether leasing will continue to save me money in the long run.

## 3. Today's Numbers

In December 2023, my monthly electricity costs were $362.59 for my Hawaiian Electric bill and $136.59 for my original Sunrun system. Together, I was paying $499.18 per month. Based on my most recent bills, my costs are now $136.59 for my original Sunrun system, $217.84 for my new solar lease, including tax, and $0.87 for my electric company bill. This brings my current total to $355.30 per month.

The difference between these two amounts is $143.88 per month. That equals approximately $1,726.56 per year if the same difference continues for 12 months. My electric company bill is now much lower, which helps offset the cost of my new solar lease. However, I still have to pay for both Sunrun systems, so it is important to include both payments when looking at my overall expenses.

One thing I need to be careful about is that this comparison uses my December 2023 costs and my most recent bill rather than a full year of data. Electricity usage can change depending on the month, household activities, and other factors. These numbers give me a starting point, but I need more information to determine whether the savings will continue over the full 25-year lease.

## 4. What Happens Next

To understand how my solar lease could affect my finances over 25 years, I compared four possible annual increases in electricity prices: 0%, 3%, 5%, and 8.4%. The 3% scenario is based on the approximate annual increase in Hawaiʻi's average electricity prices from 2010 to 2024. The 8.4% scenario reflects the faster increase from 2020 to 2024. The 0% scenario assumes electricity prices stay the same, while the 5% scenario shows what could happen if prices increase faster than the long-term average. My calculations use an average monthly electricity bill of $337.32, based on my bills from November 2023, December 2023, and January 2024.

Figure 1 shows that my estimated cumulative savings over 25 years are about $8,500 if electricity prices do not increase, $59,000 with 3% annual increases, $110,000 with 5% increases, and $247,000 with 8.4% increases. These results show how much electricity prices can affect the financial value of my solar lease. The higher electricity prices rise, the more money I could save by reducing the amount of electricity I buy from the utility.

The 0% scenario is especially important. Even if electricity prices stay flat, the lease is estimated to save money overall. However, the savings do not keep increasing throughout the contract. The lease saves money for about the first 16 years, and starting in year 17, the lease payment becomes higher than the electric bill it replaces. My cumulative savings peak at approximately $13,500 before falling to about $8,500 by the end of the 25-year period. This shows why I need to consider the timing of payments and savings, not just the monthly cost today.

Based on the model, electricity prices would need to fall by about 0.7% annually for the lease to lose money over the full 25 years. However, these estimates assume that household electricity use and solar performance remain consistent. Changes in my family's energy use, system performance, or future electricity charges could affect the actual savings. Therefore, the chart is a useful estimate, but it cannot guarantee what I will save.

## 5. Recommendation

Based on my research so far, I believe leasing solar can be a good option for homeowners in Hawaiʻi, but they should look at the full cost before signing a long-term contract. In my case, my current monthly costs are lower than they were in December 2023, and my 25-year model estimates savings even if electricity prices stay the same. The model estimates about $8,500 in total savings with no electricity price increases, compared with about $59,000 if prices increase by 3% annually. However, the flat-price scenario shows that savings can change over time, with the lease payment becoming higher than the electric bill it replaces around year 17. I would recommend leasing if the expected savings justify the payments, the contract terms are affordable, and the homeowner plans to stay long enough to benefit. I would be more cautious if the contract has large increases, the home may be sold soon, or the numbers show little savings.

## 6. The Strongest Objection

The strongest argument against my recommendation is that a 25-year solar lease is a major commitment, and the savings are not guaranteed. My calculations depend on assumptions about electricity prices, household energy use, and how well the solar system performs over time. If my family uses more electricity, the system produces less than expected, or the lease terms create costs I did not account for, my actual savings could be lower than the chart predicts. Also, even in the flat-price scenario, the lease payment becomes higher than the electric bill it replaces around year 17, which shows that the monthly comparison can change over time. My response is that these risks do not automatically make leasing a bad choice, but they do mean homeowners should review the full contract, compare leasing with buying a system or staying with utility electricity, and test different price assumptions before deciding. My own experience is a useful starting point, but it is not proof that leasing is best for every household.
